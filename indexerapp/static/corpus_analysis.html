Corpus analysis

Every indexed manuscript compared with every other, by the prayers it carries, the order they stand in and the rubrics they fall under. Prayers are clustered first — traditions are a property of prayers, not of books — and manuscript families follow from that. Everything is computed by a background run; this page only displays what a run has already written. Wherever a prayer is named by its CO number or its opening words, hover over it — or tap it, or tab to it — to read the whole text and what tradition it belongs to.

By default every indexed manuscript is compared. Tick a subset here to run the analysis over that alone — document frequency, rarity weighting and the clusters are all computed relative to what you select, so a narrower corpus is a different analysis, not a filtered view of this one.

This tab is the whole run written out as prose, for one comparison group at a time. It says how many manuscripts and how many prayers went in; which liturgical clusters were found and what each is made of; which prayers form the shared core of the group and which ones set individual books apart; what the order of the texts, and the runs of text shared between books, suggest about their descent; how the manuscripts fall into groups; and which witnesses refuse to join any group. The closing section, Method and caveats, states plainly what these figures will and will not bear — it is worth reading before quoting any number from this page.

How it is computed: Every indexed item that is linked to a standardized formula is loaded; manuscripts with fewer such items than the threshold above are set aside as too thin to compare. The corpus is then split into comparison groups — all manuscripts together, plus one group per liturgical genre. Within each group the prayers are clustered first and the manuscripts compared afterwards, shared runs of text and rubric behaviour are collected on top of that, and the resulting figures are turned into the sections and tables shown here. Nothing is calculated while you read: the page only displays what a completed background run has already written.

Each point is a single prayer. Points lying close together are prayers that behave alike across the corpus: they are carried by the same manuscripts, stand at a similar place in the book, and keep similar neighbours. A dense group of points is therefore a candidate tradition or liturgical cluster — in this analysis a tradition is a property of prayers, not of books, and manuscript families are derived from it afterwards. Click a point to pin it, then hover another to compare the two texts. The axes carry no units: read which points cluster with which, not how far apart they are.

How it is computed: Each prayer is described by four things — which manuscripts carry it and how often, its average position in the book and how much that position varies, a bag of the formulas standing within three occurrences on either side of it, and its rubric where one is recorded. Those blocks are weighted (co-occurrence counts most) and joined into a single descriptor, with the neighbour bag first compressed to 64 dimensions. The descriptors are clustered three ways, because they answer different questions: HDBSCAN for hard groups that may also declare a prayer an outlier, non-negative matrix factorization for the soft clusters a book can mix, and agglomerative clustering for a hierarchy. The same descriptors are then projected into the three dimensions you see here with t-SNE, and every clustering is scored against the traditions already recorded in the database.

Every manuscript of the comparison group set against every other. One cell is one pair, and the darker it is the more the two books have in common by the metric chosen above. The rows and columns are not in catalogue order: they are ordered by the clustering, so groups of related manuscripts appear as dark squares along the diagonal, while a book that belongs with none of the others shows up as a pale row crossing the whole grid. Click a cell to see what that particular pair actually shares.

How it is computed: Each manuscript is reduced to the sequence of its standardized formulas, repetitions kept, since a prayer said three times in one book is three occurrences. Every pair is then compared on content — Jaccard and weighted Jaccard, containment (is one book largely inside the other?), and a cosine similarity in which rarer prayers weigh more than common ones — and on order — longest common subsequence, Kendall's tau-b, breakpoint rate, and the proportion of shared adjacent pairs. The matrix for the metric you select is turned into distances and clustered hierarchically, and that clustering fixes the order of the rows and columns. The rarity-weighted cosine is the default, because two books sharing an uncommon prayer is far stronger evidence than two books sharing a universal one.

The same pairwise comparison, drawn as a map rather than a grid. Each point is one manuscript, and the distance between two points approximates how unlike their contents are: neighbours share repertoire, distant points do not. The colouring can be switched between the dominant tradition recorded for the book, the group the analysis itself discovered, the dominant liturgical cluster, and the date — which is the quickest way to ask whether the groups emerging from the data agree with what is already known about these manuscripts, or cut across it.

How it is computed: The similarity matrix for the chosen metric is converted into distances, and classical multidimensional scaling (principal coordinates analysis) finds the two-dimensional arrangement that preserves those distances as faithfully as two dimensions allow. Each point is then coloured by the attribute selected. Because the flattening is lossy, treat neighbourhoods as meaningful and exact coordinates as not; the dendrogram and the NeighborNet/SplitsTree files written by the same run are the stricter view of the same distances.

The raw evidence underneath everything else on this page. Every row is one prayer, every column one manuscript, and a mark means that manuscript carries that prayer. Neither rows nor columns stand in their original order: both have been rearranged so that prayers shared by the same witnesses end up next to one another. What then appears are solid blocks — repertoire that a group of books holds in common — against ragged edges of material peculiar to a single witness. It is the seriation diagram long used for dating archaeological assemblages, applied to liturgical books, and it shows the evidence itself rather than a summary of it.

How it is computed: A presence/absence table of prayers against manuscripts is built from the indexed content. The columns are put in the order of the hierarchical clustering of the manuscripts, so related books stand side by side. The rows are then sorted by the pattern of witnesses that carry them — first by the earliest witness, then by the full pattern, then by the prayer's average position in the book — so that identical patterns become adjacent and block structure emerges without any further processing. The grid is drawn to a canvas because a full corpus reaches millions of cells. Nothing is smoothed or inferred: every mark is one indexed occurrence.

A manuscript is rarely one pure tradition. Each bar breaks one book down into the liturgical clusters the analysis discovered, showing how much of its content is drawn from each. This is the measurable form of a statement such as “Gregorian with a Gelasian supplement”: the clusters are allowed to mix, which is how the sources actually behave, whereas assigning each book to a single family is not. The table below names each cluster by the prayers that weigh most heavily in it — the analysis finds the clusters but cannot name them, so deciding what a cluster is remains the historian's judgement. Note that a cluster is a set of prayers that travel together, with no regard to the order they stand in: it says what a book draws on, not how it is arranged. Order is treated separately, under Seriation and in the shared runs of text reported on the Report tab.

How it is computed: A table of prayer counts per manuscript is built, each prayer weighted by its rarity, so that widespread texts do not drown out the distinctive ones. Only presence and frequency enter that table — never position or sequence. Non-negative matrix factorization splits it in two: how much of each cluster every manuscript draws on, and which prayers define every cluster. The number of clusters is not chosen in advance — factorizations from two upwards (to at most twelve, and never more than the number of manuscripts allows) are compared, and the one at the elbow is kept, that is the point past which extra clusters stop explaining much more. Each manuscript's weights are finally normalised into shares that add up to the whole book, and the clusters are scored against the traditions already recorded in the database.
